# ⚡ 15-Min Sprint: The Decomposition Framework

Framework
🗣️ 15-SECOND INTERVIEW ANSWER

"Deliver a functioning, simple idea first and verbally agree on the plan with your interviewer before diving into high-level design; jumping straight to technical implementation is the #1 rejection failure mode in FDE decomposition rounds."

Palantir defines Problem Decomposition as: 'technical challenges that are open-ended in nature, with multiple possible solutions that incur different trade-offs.' Every prompt follows an invariant structure: a named institution + a loosely-bounded measurable problem + messy legacy data sources + zero technical spec.

Verbatim Drills

# 1. Cooking for Elderly People with Poor Vision (Verbatim Palantir Prompt)

📐 WORKED PROBLEM

Palantir Verbatim Prompt: Technology for Visually Impaired Elderly to Cook Safely

Step 1 Clarify the Human Constraint
The user is elderly, has severe vision loss (cataracts/macular degeneration), may have mild tremors or cognitive fatigue, and is working around high heat, sharp knives, and boiling liquids.
Step 2 Frame the Minimal Viable Solution (MVP)
Avoid complex computer vision headsets that disorient elderly users. Propose a smart tactile induction cooktop with physical rotary dial clicks, coupled with a high-contrast voice-prompt scale and RFID-tagged spice jars.
Step 3 Architect the Hardware/Software Boundary
Spices and pantry containers have distinct tactile shapes and high-frequency RFID tags. Placing a container on the prep station triggers an audio readout: 'Black Pepper, 5 grams.' The induction cooktop has temperature limits and auto-shutoff if no pan is detected for 30 seconds.
Step 4 Address Failure Modes & Edge Cases
What if the user spills boiling water? The cooktop is induction (glass surface never burns). What if internet drops? The entire system operates offline on local microcontrollers with zero cloud dependencies.
👁️ Click to Reveal Final Verification & Answer
Focusing on physical human ergonomics (tactile feedback + voice + induction safety) passes with distinction, whereas proposing an AR headset with computer vision object detection fails on safety and user adoption!

# 🚨 Mid-Round Constraint Breaks: How to Re-Plan

Live Adaptation

In Palantir and FDE loops, interviewers almost always introduce a new constraint mid-round (e.g. 'The city just informed us that ambulance GPS cannot be tracked due to union contracts.'). Defending your original plan is an instant fail; gracefully acknowledging the change and narrating an alternative is an immediate pass.

🎯 INTERVIEW DRILL LADDER
Root Question: You designed an emergency 911 dispatch optimization system relying on real-time ambulance GPS feeds. The interviewer injects: 'The paramedic union has vetoed real-time GPS tracking due to privacy agreements.' How do you adapt?
↳ Follow-Up Drill 1
Q: Do not argue with the constraint.
Acknowledge immediately: 'Understood. That removes our live route tracking, but we can still optimize dispatch using historical station readiness and cell tower check-ins.'
↳ Follow-Up Drill 2
Q: What data source replaces live GPS?
We substitute automated bay-door open/close sensors at ambulance stations and radio milestone check-ins (Unit En Route, On Scene, Transporting).
↳ Follow-Up Drill 3
Q: How does this impact the accuracy metric?
We transition from dynamic sub-minute routing to static zone-based readiness scores, while preserving 85% of our dispatch optimization value.

# 🔍 Re-Engineering Round & Red Herrings

Debugging Drill
⚠️ INTERVIEW TRAP The Re-Engineering Round Trap: Read ALL Code Before Touching Anything!
In the Re-Engineering round, you are given 200-500 lines of complex unfamiliar code with a buggy output. Interviewers deliberately plant red herrings (misleading stylistic quirks, irrelevant off-by-one loops, or dead functions). If you jump at the first suspicious line you see, you waste 20 minutes in a rabbit hole. Read the entire codebase from top to bottom, write down input/output expectations, and trace data flow before changing a single character.
